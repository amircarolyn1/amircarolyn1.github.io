I study how early-life drug exposure shapes the developing brain, using machine learning and neuroimaging to predict psychiatric and substance use outcomes before illness takes hold.

I am a Kirschstein-NRSA T32 Postdoctoral Fellow at Harvard Medical School and Boston Children's Hospital. I completed my PhD in Neuroscience at UCLA with Dr. Carrie Bearden, supported by an NIH F31 fellowship, where my dissertation, "From Synapse to Syndrome: Substance Use, Psychosis, and the Neural Architecture of Risk", integrated neuroimaging, clinical, and computational approaches to predict substance use and psychosis outcomes in over 11,000 participants. Before that, I was a postbaccalaureate research fellow at the NIH studying the neural correlates of pain and expectation, and an undergraduate researcher at Boston University working on EEG and brain stimulation.

My current work characterizes female-specific windows of cannabis-related neurodevelopmental risk, from prenatal exposure effects on infant brain development (HBCD Study) to pubertal hormone–cannabis interactions shaping psychiatric risk in girls.

Outside the lab, I hold a B.A. minor in Japanese Language and Culture and speak Japanese at a professional working level.

Research

Cannabis exposure across neurodevelopment and psychosis risk

Establishing the neurodevelopmental pathways linking cannabis exposure during sensitive windows to psychosis, including among the first evidence tying prenatal exposure to a specific neural mechanism of psychotic-like experiences in youth, a comprehensive mechanistic review, and a clinically translated harm reduction framework for adolescents at high risk.

Predictive modeling of psychiatric outcomes

Building machine learning pipelines that integrate neuroimaging, clinical, and environmental data to predict which youth are at greatest risk for hazardous substance use, psychosis-spectrum symptoms, and their co-occurrence, and applying entropy measures to characterize neural complexity in early psychosis.

Sex differences in neuroimmune function and psychiatric risk

Identifying sex-differentiated inflammatory profiles in carriers of 22q11.2 copy number variants, and characterizing substance use in youth at genetic and clinical high risk for psychosis, the first exploration of substance use risk factors in 22q11.2 deletion syndrome.

Pain, expectation, and reliability in human research

First evidence that female participants are more reliable than males on all pain sensitivity measures, refuting assumptions historically used to exclude women from pain research; studies of how provider appearance and social perception shape pain and treatment expectations.
